THE PROBLEM THIS IS TRYING TO SOLVE
----------------------------------------------------------------

Getting groups to genuine decisions is slow. Not because
people are difficult — because the process usually is.

Workshops surface the loudest voices. Surveys flatten
nuance into numbers. Consultations produce reports nobody
reads. And somewhere in the middle, the thing people
actually needed — to feel heard, to understand each other,
and to land somewhere everyone can commit to — gets lost.

The result is decisions that look like consensus but
aren't. People leave the room with reservations they never
voiced. Change programmes stall. The same conversation
happens again six months later.


----------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THE RESEARCH SHOWS
----------------------------------------------------------------

In 2024, Google DeepMind published a study in the journal
Science. They worked with over 5,000 people on genuinely
contested topics — the kind where people hold strong and
opposing views.

They found that an AI-assisted process consistently
produced statements that more people genuinely endorsed
than those produced by traditional human facilitation.
Not because the AI replaced the facilitator — but because
it could do something no human can do at speed:

  Read every single person's view carefully.
  Hold all of it in mind simultaneously.
  Draft candidate statements that try to honour
  the concerns on all sides.
  Predict how each person would rank those options.
  Select the one with the broadest genuine support.

Then give that to a human facilitator as a starting point,
not a conclusion.

The key word is genuine. The process is specifically
designed to find formulations that people across different
positions can accept — not statements that win a majority
while leaving a significant minority cold.


----------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT I'VE ADDED
----------------------------------------------------------------

The DeepMind approach produces a refined consensus
statement. That's valuable. But it doesn't tell you much
about the shape of opinion in the room — who thinks like
whom, where the real fault lines are, and which ideas
genuinely bridge the divides versus which ones just happen
to win the vote.

I've added an opinion mapping layer that does exactly that.

After the initial deliberation, participants spend two
minutes rating a shortlist of statements. The system then
groups people by how similarly they voted — not by their
job title, their team, or anything else. Just by the
actual pattern of what they agreed and disagreed on.

The result is a simple map showing:

  WHERE THE GROUPS ARE
  Who tends to see things similarly, and how distinct
  the groups are from each other.

  WHAT BRIDGES THEM
  Statements that every group could accept — genuine
  common ground, not just a majority position.

  WHERE THE REAL TENSION IS
  Statements that one group strongly supports and another
  strongly rejects. These are the conversations worth
  having, because they're the ones that will surface
  anyway — better to surface them deliberately.

A human facilitator walking into a room with this
information already knows the landscape. Instead of
spending the first hour discovering where people stand,
they can go straight to the work that matters.


----------------------------------------------------------------
WHY THIS IS USEFUL FOR CHANGE WORK
----------------------------------------------------------------

Most of the resistance that slows change programmes down
isn't irrational. It's unexpressed. People have concerns
they didn't voice in the consultation, needs that weren't
captured in the survey, and a general sense that nobody
quite understood what they were trying to say.

This process is specifically designed to surface that.
The question it asks isn't "do you support this?" —
it's "what matters to you, and what would make this
unworkable?" That distinction changes what you get back.

It also scales. The deep deliberation phase works well
with groups of 5 to 20. The opinion mapping can handle
hundreds. You could run an intensive session with team
leads and then circulate the resulting statements to the
whole department to map where the broader population sits.

THE ASK
----------------------------------------------------------------

I'm looking for a small number of people willing to try
the process on something real.

It doesn't need to be a high-stakes decision. It just
needs to be a question where a group of people genuinely
have different views and need to land somewhere together.
A team priority, a ways-of-working question, a policy
discussion — anything that would normally require a
workshop or a lengthy email chain.

Your involvement across all three stages would take about
10 minutes of your time, spread over a week or so.

In return, you'd get:
  - A clear picture of where your group's opinion
    landscape actually is
  - A set of statements reflecting genuine common ground
  - A facilitator brief you could use immediately
  - A candid debrief on how well the process worked
    and where it fell short

This is an experiment, not a product. I'll be honest
about what it does well and what still needs work.
